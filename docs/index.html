Building digital twins from survey marginals

Turn published toplines and marginal distributions into a calibrated mixture of synthetic respondent profiles—without pretending aggregate data contains respondent-level records.

Tool: umrissRuntime: Python + EDSLInterface: JSON-first CLI

01 The problem

Many survey reports publish distributions for each question but withhold the underlying microdata. Those marginals describe a population, yet they do not reveal how answers co-occur within people. umriss builds an explicit approximation: a reusable bank of plausible synthetic respondents and a set of nonnegative weights chosen to reproduce the observed marginals.

The method does not ask a model to invent a finished synthetic population in one step. It separates the task into target recording, support construction, measurement, and calibration. That separation matters because it lets us inspect which assumptions come from the report, which come from the synthetic profiles, and which come from the weighting procedure.

First, record the targets

For every survey item, we record the exact question wording, the answer options in their published order, and the reported proportion selecting each option. These proportions are the calibration targets. If a report says that 21 percent have “a great deal” of confidence in the courts, 55 percent have “some,” and 24 percent have “hardly any,” the vector (0.21, 0.55, 0.24) becomes the target for that item.

At this stage there are no synthetic respondents and no assumptions about correlations between questions. The metadata simply preserves what the report actually tells us. Keeping this boundary clean prevents model-generated beliefs from being mistaken for observed evidence.

Second, create broad support

We then create a bank of synthetic respondent types, called support points. A support point is not meant to be a recovered individual or a demographic estimate. It is a basis function: one coherent way that answers across the battery might move together.

The initial bank is designed to cover the response space broadly. Some support points anchor explicit answer patterns; others target underrepresented item-option regions or response styles. This is the “uniform” starting point in the workflow: before calibration, every valid support point receives equal weight. It does not mean that every synthetic respondent answers every option with equal probability. It means that the bank begins without declaring one synthetic type more prevalent than another.

Third, measure each synthetic type

Each support prompt asks the model for a complete probability vector on every item. A row might assign high probability to trusting the courts, moderate probability to trusting the police, and low probability to trusting parliament. Collecting the full vector preserves uncertainty and creates a numerical matrix that can be calibrated; a single hard answer would throw away useful information.

With equal weights, averaging the rows gives the unweighted support-bank prediction. This is an important baseline. It shows what the designed synthetic population predicts before seeing the reported target proportions.

Fourth, reweight the bank

Calibration changes the prevalence of the synthetic types, not their recorded answer probabilities. If a support point helps reproduce several reported marginals simultaneously, its weight can rise. If it pushes the mixture away from the targets, its weight can fall. All weights remain nonnegative and sum to one, so the fitted result remains an interpretable mixture of the original support points.

Entropy regularization keeps the fit near the equal-weight starting point unless the targets provide a reason to move away from it. Without that restraint, a flexible bank could match the observed marginals by putting nearly all weight on a few rows. Such a solution might interpolate well but represent a fragile synthetic population.

Finally, test what was not fitted

Matching the marginals used during calibration is necessary but not persuasive: those values were the fitting targets. The meaningful test withholds one item, fits the weights using all remaining items, and predicts the omitted item from the same weighted support bank. Repeating this for every item measures whether the support bank captures reusable cross-item structure.

Marginals alone do not identify a unique joint distribution. Support design and regularization encode assumptions, so the result should be described as a calibrated synthetic population rather than a set of recovered respondents. The essential empirical question is whether those assumptions help predict omitted marginals better than simple alternatives.

02 Mixture calibration

The support bank turns the qualitative idea above into a matrix. Let B contain support-point probabilities: rows are synthetic profiles and columns are item-option cells. With 12 support points and four three-option items, B has 12 rows and 12 columns. Each group of three cells in a row sums to one.

Let y contain the reported marginal probabilities for the items used in a fit. Let b be the starting weights, which are uniform by default: each support point begins with weight 1/12. The fitted weight vector π lies on the simplex, so its entries are nonnegative and sum to one.

\[ \underset{\boldsymbol{\pi}}{\operatorname{minimize}}\quad \left\lVert B_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\pi} - \mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{in}}\right\rVert_2^2 + \rho\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\pi}\,\middle\|\,\mathbf{b}\right) \] \[ \text{subject to}\qquad \pi_s \ge 0,\qquad \sum_{s=1}^{S}\pi_s = 1 \] Fit the reported held-in marginals while penalizing concentration away from the base weights.

The first term measures the discrepancy between the mixture prediction and the recorded targets. The second term measures how far the fitted weights move from the equal-weight base. The parameter ρ controls the tradeoff: smaller values prioritize a close marginal fit, while larger values preserve a more diffuse synthetic population.

umriss tries a small default grid of ρ values. It selects a value using held-in fit plus a penalty for low effective support; the held-out outcome is never used for this choice. The diagnostics record the selected ρ, held-in residual, maximum weight, top-ten weight share, convergence status, and effective support.

\[ \widehat{\mathbf{y}}_h = B_h^{\mathsf T}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}} \] Predict held-out item h by applying the calibrated weights to its support probabilities.

The same fitted weights are applied to the support probabilities for the omitted item. No additional model call occurs at prediction time. The prediction comes entirely from cross-item structure already present in the support bank and the weights learned from the held-in targets.

04 Describe the survey battery

Begin by authoring the survey metadata incrementally in a managed workspace. This is where published evidence becomes machine-readable calibration targets. The commands bind each item to its exact wording, ordered answer options, and observed marginals.

The option order is part of the data. Every later probability vector uses this same order, so changing it would silently associate probabilities with the wrong labels. The proportions should reflect the same population, weighting convention, and denominator across the battery. If the report uses different bases for different questions, record that distinction in the surrounding provenance rather than treating the vectors as directly comparable.

05 Design the support bank

The support bank supplies candidate patterns of cross-item dependence. If every row expresses the same outlook, no weighting scheme can manufacture a missing response pattern. The first bank should therefore prioritize coverage rather than realism about population prevalence. Prevalence is what calibration will estimate.

The built-in pattern-coverage strategy combines response-pattern anchors with item-option coverage rows. Pattern anchors ask for coherent synthetic types around different answer scaffolds. Coverage rows deliberately visit particular response regions—for example, a type for whom “hardly any” confidence in the courts is natural—while asking the model to carry the underlying outlook coherently across the other institutions.

These prompts ask for probabilistic rather than deterministic answers. A support point can lean strongly toward one option while retaining uncertainty. Once all 12 rows are measured, they initially receive equal weight. The unweighted bank is therefore a broad reference population, not a claim that the 12 types are equally common in reality.

umriss support build \
  --battery trust \

At the end of support build, umriss has therefore proposed twelve things to measure. It has not created a fitted twin population, assigned weights, or called a model. support export packages these rendered prompts as EDSL jobs; ep run is the step that obtains a persona and four probability vectors for each support point.

Why --battery rather than a JSON path?

The tutorial already authored trust inside the active .umriss project, so --battery trust resolves to .umriss/projects/default/batteries/trust/battery.json. There is no need to compile and pass the same JSON file again.

The alternative --metadata path.json form is for stateless use: an agent may already have a complete metadata artifact, may be running in automation without a workspace, or may want to evaluate a read-only battery without importing it. Exactly one of --battery and --metadata is required.

The metadata_source field confirms that the managed battery was used. The command is deterministic: it writes 12 prompt records and a design table but makes no model calls. The JSONL contains the full prompt for each support point. The design CSV makes the intended coverage visible without requiring you to read every prompt in full.

Inspect before model execution

Read the generated JSONL and design CSV before spending on inference. Look for duplicated profiles, unsupported demographic claims, leading language, option-order mistakes, and axes that merely restate the known marginals.

After establishing a pattern-coverage baseline, you can compare it with theory-driven banks. A generic design file can cross latent outlooks with response styles and sample that space using a fixed seed. Such a bank may encode better domain structure, but it also adds assumptions and should be evaluated against the same held-out items.

06 Run model jobs at the EDSL boundary

The prompt file describes the support population, but it does not yet contain measured answer probabilities. support export converts each prompt row into an EDSL scenario and combines those scenarios with a question requiring the probability JSON. The resulting .jobs.ep object records the exact prompts, model configuration, and scenario count.

umriss prepares jobs but never executes frontier-model calls. This division keeps credentials, service selection, retries, waiting, and remote failures outside deterministic analysis commands. It also prevents a later parse or fit command from silently rerunning a failed row with different settings.

07 Parse and inspect the support bank

The registered table still contains model-generated JSON. Parsing turns it into the numerical support matrix used by calibration. For each support point, the parser checks that every battery item is present, that the number of probabilities matches the ordered options, that values are nonnegative, and that each vector has positive mass that can be normalized.

The output is a long table with one row per support point, item, and option. For 12 support points and four three-option items, a complete bank contains 144 probability rows. Keeping the table long makes option ordering and missing cells easier to audit than a dense array.

08 Fit weights and predict

At the start of fitting, all 12 valid support points have equal weight. Their simple average is the unweighted bank prediction. The optimizer then searches for a new nonnegative weight vector whose weighted average matches the recorded marginals for the held-in items, while the entropy penalty resists unnecessary movement away from equal weights.

Fit on all available items when the goal is a final calibrated synthetic population. Exclude an item when testing whether weights learned elsewhere transfer to an unseen target. In this example, the courts marginal is deliberately omitted; the police, parliament, and civil-service marginals determine the weights.

09 Leave-one-item-out validation

The manual courts holdout demonstrates one prediction. Leave-one-item-out evaluation automates the same procedure across the battery. For each item in turn, umriss hides its target vector, selects ρ using only the remaining targets, fits weights, predicts the hidden vector, and computes root mean squared error after revealing the truth.

Every holdout is compared with two inexpensive baselines. The unweighted support bank gives every synthetic type equal prevalence and asks whether calibration improved on the original broad bank. The uniform response baseline assigns equal probability to every answer option, such as one third per option in this example. These are different notions of uniformity: equal weights across twins versus equal probability across responses.

12 Completion checklist

  1. Record the report, population, field dates, weighting, question wording, and ordered options.
  2. Validate that each marginal is complete, nonnegative, and normalized.
  3. Declare support designs and random seeds before inspecting held-out results.
  4. Inspect generated prompts and preserve the exact EDSL jobs and results objects.
  5. Review parse failures and support diversity before fitting.
  6. Report held-in fit, effective support, held-out accuracy, and design sensitivity together.
  7. State clearly that the calibrated joint distribution is model-dependent and not identified by marginals alone.
  8. Archive report tables and the reproducibility manifest with the final interpretation.

A run is complete when another analyst can trace every reported number back through calibration weights, support probabilities, registered model results, prompts, design configuration, and source marginals.
